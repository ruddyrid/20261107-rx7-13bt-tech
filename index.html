01 / Family

Two series, one eccentric heart

The U.S. FC Turbo II ran the 13B-T from 1987 to 1991. Series 5 is not a different engine — it is a systematic update: more compression, higher published boost pressure, revised turbo control, and electronic oil metering. The long-block architecture stayed the same.

02 / Specifications

Series 4 vs Series 5

Same 1,308 cc twin-rotor long block, different calibration and hardware. The big changes are compression (8.5 to 9.0), the published boost-pressure specification (45.2 to 57.0 kPa), and the switching-valve/control arrangement. Both turbos are described as twin-scroll.

03 / Power

Where the 200 hp comes from

A rotary makes torque from airflow and rpm, not from displacement. The 13B-T's power curve is flat and broad, but the limit is detonation — not the turbo's ability to make boost.

182 → 200 hp
+10% power, +26% FSM pressure spec

Series 4: twin-scroll with a quick-spool valve. The 8.5:1 rotors and the FSM's 45.2 kPa (6.56 psi) boost-pressure specification pair with a vacuum-operated switching valve. The FSM actuator check says rod movement begins above roughly 2,700 rpm; the exact relationship between that test and manifold boost is not established here.

Series 5: higher compression and revised boost control. Mazda raised compression to 9.0:1 and lists boost pressure as 57.0 kPa (8.25 psi printed). Both series use a twin-scroll turbo; the S5 uses a divided manifold without the S4 switching valve and controls boost with a wastegate and duty-cycle solenoid. Turbo model suffixes and detailed pulse routing are not specified by the cited FSM pages.

The rotary caveat. Detonation and excess heat are critical risks. Intake temperature, fuel quality, oil metering, and ECU calibration matter alongside boost pressure. A boost controller on a stock ECU is not a power upgrade — it can reduce the engine's detonation margin.

04 / Blueprint

The geometry of a Wankel

A 13B is two rotors on one eccentric shaft, each chasing a two-lobe trochoid. The numbers below are the ones that matter when you are checking wear, ordering seals, or explaining why a rotary is not a piston engine.

Apex-groove standard: 1988 .051–.101 mm; 1989 .041–.091 mm. Side-seal-to-groove standard .028–.078 mm, limit .10 mm; side-seal-to-corner standard .05–.15 mm, limit .40 mm. Rotor-side minimum is .10 mm; end-play maximum .09 mm. Apex-groove limit .15 mm both years.

How the geometry works: the rotor has three flanks but the housing has two lobes. The rotor turns at one-third of eccentric-shaft speed, and each face completes one intake, compression, power, and exhaust cycle per rotor revolution (three eccentric-shaft revolutions). That is why a 1.3 L rotary breathes like a 2.6 L four-stroke in airflow terms — and why heat and sealing dominate the build.

05 / Torque

Fastener values that matter

Click any value to copy it. Thread preparation is fastener-specific; the FSM says not to oil wheel lug nuts. The big three on a rotary are the tension bolts, stationary gears, and flywheel nut — verify each value in the procedure for your model year.

The flywheel/counterweight nut is the one that bites. 290–360 ft-lb on a 13B requires the factory holding tool and a torque wrench rated for the value. The FSM specifies thread-locking compound on the shaft threads and sealant on the nut face; a new nut is builder practice, not an FSM instruction.

06 / Rebuild

From bare housings to first start

A 13B rebuild is a sealing job, not a machining job. The stack has to be clean, the seals have to be lubricated, and the tension bolts have to be torqued evenly. Work through the stages in order and check the box when the step is genuinely done.

8 stages
stock rebuild
Rebuild progress
0 / 8
01 Teardown & inspectionBefore you buy parts
  1. Compression-test the engine before it comes apart. Record all three faces of both rotors — a single low face changes the diagnosis.
  2. Remove the turbo, intake, exhaust, front cover, and accessories. Bag and label the fasteners by assembly; the tension bolts are not interchangeable with cover bolts.
  3. Keep the rotor housings and side plates paired and marked. Mixing worn parts across a stack is how you end up with a clearance you cannot explain.
  4. Inspect the hard-chrome-plated housing faces for flaking, chatter, and apex-seal track wear. Check the side housings for seal-track grooves and coolant-seal erosion.
Compression ≥ 85 psi / face @ 250 rpmMax chamber spread 21 psiOil pressure 64–78 psi @ 3,000 rpm warm

Why it matters: a rotary that failed a compression test rarely needs only seals. The housings and side plates are the expensive parts — inspect them before you order a seal kit.

02 Measure & decideReuse or replace
  1. Measure rotor and housing widths at several points against the year-specific FSM dimensions. Measure side-housing wear and rotor side clearance with the rotor in the housing.
  2. Check the apex-seal groove width and the side-seal groove. A worn groove will not hold a new seal at the right height.
  3. Measure eccentric shaft end play and inspect the journals for scoring, taper, and brinelling. Check the stationary gear teeth for pitting.
  4. Decide what is reusable before ordering. A seal kit plus gaskets is normal; a shaft, housings, or side plates are a different budget.
Rotor 79.800–79.850 mmHousing 79.970–80.010 mmHousing width difference ≤ 0.06 mmRotor side 0.12–0.21 mm std; 0.10 mm minApex groove: 1988 .051–.101 mm; 1989 .041–.091 mmEnd play .040–.070 mm std

Why it matters: seal kits are cheap; housings are not. Measuring first is the difference between a rebuild and a parts cannon.

03 Rotor assemblySeals & springs
  1. Clean the rotor thoroughly and blow out every seal groove. Any grit left in a groove will hold a seal off its seat.
  2. Install the apex seals and springs, then the corner seals and springs. Fit the side seals and springs last, and fit the oil seal rings to the rotor sides.
  3. Lubricate every seal and groove with clean engine oil or assembly lube. Check that each seal projects correctly and moves freely in its groove.
  4. Keep the assembled rotor covered and clean while you prepare the shaft and housings.
Apex seal 2.0 mm nominal; actual 1.910–1.939 mmCorner projection ≥ 0.50 mm minimumSide-seal groove limit 0.10 mmSide-to-corner limit 0.40 mmApex springs: long 6.25 / 4.6 mm; short 3.3 / 1.7 mm std / limit

Why it matters: a dry seal will scuff on the first start. Lube everything, keep grit out, and do not force a seal into a groove it does not fit.

04 Eccentric shaft & bearingsThe rotary crank
  1. Fit the main bearings into the stationary gears and the rotor bearing into the rotor hub. Check that the bearing shells are seated and the oil holes line up.
  2. Inspect the eccentric shaft journals for scoring and taper. Check the eccentric lobe and the thrust surfaces.
  3. Lubricate the journals and bearing faces, then trial-fit the shaft and rotor. The rotor should turn smoothly by hand with no tight spot.
  4. Check end play with a dial indicator before final assembly and confirm it against the FSM.
No journal scoringRotor bearing no brinellingEnd play 0.0016–0.0028 in

Why it matters: the rotor bearing is a heavily loaded needle bearing. Inspect it for brinelling and follow the FSM inspection/replacement criteria; the cited sources do not establish that it is the engine's highest-load bearing.

05 Stack & sealHousings, plates, bolts
  1. Dry-stack the front side plate, front rotor housing and rotor, intermediate (center) housing, rear rotor housing and rotor, and rear side plate. Confirm the order and dowel pins before any sealant goes on; front and rear covers are fitted afterward, not included in the tension-bolt stack.
  2. Apply the FSM-specified sealant to the side housings only where required. More sealant is not better — excess can block an oil passage.
  3. Lower the stack together, fit the tension bolts, and run them in by hand until they seat. Do not use the bolts to pull the stack down.
  4. Check that the rotor still turns freely and that the housings are fully seated before final torque.
Sealant per FSMTension bolts 23–29 ft-lbDry-stack first

Why it matters: the stack is a sandwich of seals, not a gasket. Sealant goes only where the manual says, and the bolts should clamp a seated stack — not drag it into place.

06 Torque the stack18 bolts · factory sequence
  1. Fit new tension-bolt seal washers, oil the washers and threads, then tighten all 18 tension bolts gradually in the numbered factory sequence. The 1988 FSM identifies the marked bolt at position 17; the 1989 FSM also identifies the tubed bolt at position 18. Do not substitute a generic crisscross pattern.
  2. Final torque is 23–29 ft-lb. Confirm the exact sequence and value in the FSM for your model year.
  3. Check that the rotor turns freely by hand and that the eccentric shaft end play is still in spec. If it tightened up, stop and find out why.
  4. The cited FSM procedure gives no tension-bolt stretch or length limit. Inspect for damage and use the applicable model-year inspection procedure; do not infer a stretch limit.
23–29 ft-lb (31–39 N·m)Numbered order; tighten graduallyThree passes: shop method, not FSM instructionEnd play .040–.070 mm std

Why it matters: the tension bolts clamp the stack. Rotor side clearance is determined by machined widths and verified by measurement; uneven torque can distort the stack and bind the rotor.

07 Front cover, oil pump, OMPOil system & accessories
  1. Fit the front cover with a new seal, then the oil pump and its sprocket. Check pump clearance and prime the pump with clean oil.
  2. Install the oil metering pump and connect the lines. On a Series 4, check the mechanical rod adjustment; on a Series 5, check the stepper motor and wiring.
  3. Fit the pulley boss and eccentric shaft lock bolt, then the front pulley and stiffener. Hold the flywheel for the separate pulley-boss lock bolt; torque the M6 pulley bolts evenly to the FSM value and check runout before the belt goes on.
  4. Pre-oil the engine by spinning the oil pump or cranking with the ignition disabled until pressure is seen. Do not dry-crank a fresh rotary.
Oil pump 5.1–7.2 ft-lbOMP 5.8–8.0 ft-lbM6 pulley bolts 5.8–8.0 ft-lb (FSM)Pulley-boss lock bolt 80–98 ft-lb

Why it matters: pre-oil before the first start. A dry rotary makes metal on the first revolution, and the damage is done before the gauge moves.

08 Flywheel, turbo, first startHeat cycle & checks
  1. Apply thread-locking compound to the eccentric-shaft threads and sealant to the flywheel/counterweight nut contact face. Fit the nut (a new nut is builder practice, not an FSM instruction) and torque to 290–360 ft-lb with the factory holding tool. Install the clutch and pressure plate with an alignment tool.
  2. Fit the turbo, manifold, downpipe, and intake. Use new gaskets, support the turbo while torquing, and check every boost hose for a proper seat.
  3. Prime the oil system, disable the ignition, and crank for pressure. Then start and set idle per the year-specific FSM procedure (S4: 725–775 rpm with BAC valve; S5: 750 ±25 rpm with test connector grounded), watching oil pressure and for coolant or oil leaks.
  4. Heat-cycle the engine, recheck exhaust fasteners, then compression-test and boost-leak-check before any load. The FSM does not call for re-torquing tension bolts after heat cycling.
Flywheel 290–360 ft-lbSpark plugs 9.4–13 ft-lbFSM boost 45.2 / 57.0 kPa

Why it matters: the first start is a leak check, not a dyno pull. Idle, verify oil pressure, heat-cycle, and only then load the engine.

07 / Reference

Ignition, fluids, and the rotary tells

The 13B fires two rotors through four plugs: leading and trailing on each. The leading plug starts the burn; the trailing plug finishes it. Get the plug positions, base timing, and oil metering right before you chase a boost problem.

Compression numbers follow the factory test: 85 psi minimum per face at ~250 rpm, with no more than 21 psi spread between chambers. Correct for cranking speed and altitude; these are service limits, not a universal healthy-engine range. Base timing must be set with the check connector grounded and the engine at operating temperature. Oil: a rotary consumes oil by design through the OMP — check the level at every fuel stop, not every oil change.
