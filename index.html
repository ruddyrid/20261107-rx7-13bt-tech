01 / Family

Two series, one eccentric heart

The FC Turbo II ran the 13B-T from 1986 to 1991. Series 5 is not a different engine — it is a systematic update: more compression, more boost, a simpler turbo, and electronic oil metering. The long-block architecture stayed the same.

Read the series, not just the year. US ratings are SAE net. JDM Series 4 was rated 185 PS and Series 5 205 PS; the Infini IV was 215 PS. Boost figures are factory wastegate settings and move with altitude, intake temperature, and ECU calibration.

02 / Specifications

Series 4 vs Series 5

Same 1,308 cc twin-rotor long block, different calibration and hardware. The big jumps are compression (8.5 to 9.0), boost (5.5 to 7.5 psi), and the switch from a twin-scroll to a larger single-scroll turbo.

03 / Power

Where the 200 hp comes from

A rotary makes torque from airflow and rpm, not from displacement. The 13B-T's power curve is flat and broad, but the limit is detonation — not the turbo's ability to make boost.

182 → 200 hp
+10% power, +36% boost

Series 4: the twin-scroll years. The 8.5:1 rotors and 5.5 psi wastegate make a small, responsive turbo that builds boost early. The twin-scroll housing separates the exhaust pulses from the two rotors, which sharpens spool at the cost of a more complex control diaphragm and plumbing.

Series 5: compression and a bigger turbo. Mazda raised compression to 9.0:1 and boost to 7.5 psi, then simplified the turbo to a larger single-scroll unit. The result is more midrange and top-end, but less detonation margin on pump fuel and a stronger dependence on the stock knock control and intercooler.

The rotary caveat. Peak power is airflow-limited, but the failure mode is almost always detonation or heat, not a lack of boost. Stock ECU fuel cut, intake temperature, fuel octane, and oil metering matter more than the wastegate setting. A boost controller on a stock ECU is not a power upgrade — it is a way to find the weakest seal.

04 / Blueprint

The geometry of a Wankel

A 13B is two rotors on one eccentric shaft, each chasing a two-lobe trochoid. The numbers below are the ones that matter when you are checking wear, ordering seals, or explaining why a rotary is not a piston engine.

How the geometry works: the rotor has three flanks but the housing has two lobes. The rotor turns at one-third of the eccentric shaft speed, and each face sees one intake, compression, power, and exhaust event per eccentric shaft revolution. That is why a 1.3 L rotary behaves like a larger engine in airflow terms — and why heat and sealing dominate the build.

05 / Torque

Fastener values that matter

Click any value to copy it. Values are for clean, lightly oiled threads unless noted. The big three on a rotary are the tension bolts, the stationary gears, and the flywheel nut — get those wrong and the stack will not live.

The flywheel/counterweight nut is the one that bites. 290–360 ft-lb on a 13B is not a breaker-bar-and-prayer job. Use a proper holding tool, a new nut, and a torque wrench rated for the value. Never let the eccentric shaft take the reaction through the rotor.

06 / Rebuild

From bare housings to first start

A 13B rebuild is a sealing job, not a machining job. The stack has to be clean, the seals have to be lubricated, and the tension bolts have to be torqued evenly. Work through the stages in order and check the box when the step is genuinely done.

8 stages
stock rebuild
Rebuild progress
0 / 8
01 Teardown & inspectionBefore you buy parts
  1. Compression-test the engine before it comes apart. Record all three faces of both rotors — a single low face changes the diagnosis.
  2. Remove the turbo, intake, exhaust, front cover, and accessories. Bag and label the fasteners by assembly; the tension bolts are not interchangeable with cover bolts.
  3. Keep the rotor housings and side plates paired and marked. Mixing worn parts across a stack is how you end up with a clearance you cannot explain.
  4. Inspect the chrome-carbide housing faces for flaking, chatter, and apex-seal track wear. Check the side plates for seal-track grooves and coolant-seal erosion.
Compression ≥ 85 psi / face @ 250 rpmMax chamber spread 21 psiOil pressure ≥ 30 psi hot

Why it matters: a rotary that failed a compression test rarely needs only seals. The housings and side plates are the expensive parts — inspect them before you order a seal kit.

02 Measure & decideReuse or replace
  1. Measure rotor housing width at several points and compare to the FSM limit. Measure side-housing wear and rotor side clearance with the rotor in the housing.
  2. Check the apex-seal groove width and the side-seal groove. A worn groove will not hold a new seal at the right height.
  3. Measure eccentric shaft end play and inspect the journals for scoring, taper, and brinelling. Check the stationary gear teeth for pitting.
  4. Decide what is reusable before ordering. A seal kit plus gaskets is normal; a shaft, housings, or side plates are a different budget.
Rotor side 0.002–0.007 inApex groove 0.002–0.003 inEnd play 0.0016–0.0059 in

Why it matters: seal kits are cheap; housings are not. Measuring first is the difference between a rebuild and a parts cannon.

03 Rotor assemblySeals & springs
  1. Clean the rotor thoroughly and blow out every seal groove. Any grit left in a groove will hold a seal off its seat.
  2. Install the apex seals and springs, then the corner seals and springs. Fit the side seals and springs last, and fit the oil seal rings to the rotor sides.
  3. Lubricate every seal and groove with clean engine oil or assembly lube. Check that each seal projects correctly and moves freely in its groove.
  4. Keep the assembled rotor covered and clean while you prepare the shaft and housings.
Apex seal 2.0 mmCorner projection ≥ 0.02 inSide seal max clearance 0.016 in

Why it matters: a dry seal will scuff on the first start. Lube everything, keep grit out, and do not force a seal into a groove it does not fit.

04 Eccentric shaft & bearingsThe rotary crank
  1. Fit the main bearings into the stationary gears and the rotor bearing into the rotor hub. Check that the bearing shells are seated and the oil holes line up.
  2. Inspect the eccentric shaft journals for scoring and taper. Check the eccentric lobe and the thrust surfaces.
  3. Lubricate the journals and bearing faces, then trial-fit the shaft and rotor. The rotor should turn smoothly by hand with no tight spot.
  4. Check end play with a dial indicator before final assembly and confirm it against the FSM.
No journal scoringRotor bearing no brinellingEnd play 0.0016–0.0059 in

Why it matters: the rotor bearing is the highest-load bearing in the engine. Brinelling from detonation or low oil pressure is common on a high-boost car — replace it while you are in there.

05 Stack & sealHousings, plates, bolts
  1. Dry-stack the front side plate, rotor housing, rotor, rotor housing, and rear side plate. Confirm the order and the dowel pins before any sealant goes on.
  2. Apply the FSM-specified sealant to the side housings only where required. More sealant is not better — excess can block an oil passage.
  3. Lower the stack together, fit the tension bolts, and run them in by hand until they seat. Do not use the bolts to pull the stack down.
  4. Check that the rotor still turns freely and that the housings are fully seated before final torque.
Sealant per FSMTension bolts 24–28 ft-lbDry-stack first

Why it matters: the stack is a sandwich of seals, not a gasket. Sealant goes only where the manual says, and the bolts should clamp a seated stack — not drag it into place.

06 Torque the stackThree passes, crisscross
  1. Torque the six tension bolts in three equal passes, alternating opposite bolts. Bring the stack down evenly; do not finish one side before starting the other.
  2. Final torque is 24–28 ft-lb. Confirm the exact sequence and value in the FSM for your model year.
  3. Check that the rotor turns freely by hand and that the eccentric shaft end play is still in spec. If it tightened up, stop and find out why.
  4. Check tension-bolt length if the FSM specifies a stretch limit. Replace any bolt that is out of range.
24–28 ft-lb3 passesEnd play 0.0016–0.0059 in

Why it matters: the tension bolts set rotor side clearance. Uneven torque means uneven clearance, and an uneven stack is a short-lived engine.

07 Front cover, oil pump, OMPOil system & accessories
  1. Fit the front cover with a new seal, then the oil pump and its sprocket. Check pump clearance and prime the pump with clean oil.
  2. Install the oil metering pump and connect the lines. On a Series 4, check the mechanical rod adjustment; on a Series 5, check the stepper motor and wiring.
  3. Fit the front pulley and torque it while holding the flywheel. Check pulley runout before the belt goes on.
  4. Pre-oil the engine by spinning the oil pump or cranking with the ignition disabled until pressure is seen. Do not dry-crank a fresh rotary.
Oil pump 5–7 ft-lbOMP 5.8–8 ft-lbPulley 87–116 ft-lb

Why it matters: pre-oil before the first start. A dry rotary makes metal on the first revolution, and the damage is done before the gauge moves.

08 Flywheel, turbo, first startHeat cycle & checks
  1. Fit a new flywheel/counterweight nut and torque it to 290–360 ft-lb with a holding tool. Install the clutch and pressure plate with an alignment tool.
  2. Fit the turbo, manifold, downpipe, and intake. Use new gaskets, support the turbo while torquing, and check every boost hose for a proper seat.
  3. Prime the oil system, disable the ignition, and crank for pressure. Then start and idle at 750–850 rpm, watching oil pressure and for coolant or oil leaks.
  4. Heat-cycle the engine, recheck the tension bolts and exhaust fasteners, then compression-test and boost-leak-check before any load.
Flywheel 290–360 ft-lbPlugs 11–18 ft-lbBoost 5.5 / 7.5 psi

Why it matters: the first start is a leak check, not a dyno pull. Idle, verify oil pressure, heat-cycle, and only then load the engine.

07 / Reference

Ignition, fluids, and the rotary tells

The 13B fires two rotors through four plugs: leading and trailing on each. The leading plug starts the burn; the trailing plug finishes it. Get the plug positions, base timing, and oil metering right before you chase a boost problem.
